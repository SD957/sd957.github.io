02At a glance

A PDF has two readings. Your LLM gets the one you never see.

A PDF viewer renders glyphs onto a page. An LLM app first extracts text from the same file, and the model reasons over that text. The PDF standard does not require the two to agree, and users are never shown the extracted version.

We mined the ISO 32000 object model for places where rendering and extraction can be pulled apart, and confirmed 25 extraction gaps in four families. Several hide nothing at all: the page is fully visible, yet extraction returns different words.

Every gap diverges on at least one of 16 PDF processing stacks. Every one of 7 commercial LLM services we tested is exposed to at least 12 of them.

03How it works

One file, two paths

A renderer follows glyph programs, colors, positions and visibility state. An extractor follows Unicode mappings, accessibility metadata, content-stream order and font fallbacks. A split-view PDF makes these two paths disagree.

06Results

It's the pipeline, not the model

Every service exposes at least 12 of the 25 gaps. Which ones depends on how the service turns the PDF into text, and that changes across APIs, web apps, cloud routes, local runtimes and service updates, even when the model name stays the same.

07Fingerprinting

The same canaries reveal the hidden pipeline

Every extractor breaks in its own way, so a gap is also a probe. Upload two canary PDFs to an LLM app, ask it to quote them, and the answers point to its PDF loader. Two probes separate all seven LangChain loaders we tested. A four-page canary separates all 16 parser paths in our panel.

EG12probe 1
Visible: LARK-27. The suffix XQ9 sits on a zero-height text matrix, so it never renders.
EG18probe 2
Visible: ABC, drawn by a Type 3 font with no /ToUnicode. Loaders differ in how they guess the text.

In black-box deployments, wrappers, OCR fallbacks and version drift can blur signatures. Read a match as evidence about the loader family, not an exact version.
